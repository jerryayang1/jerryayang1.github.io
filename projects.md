## Current projects

My current work focuses on the engineering and heterogeneous integration of atomically thin two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors, in particular, transition metal dichalcognides (TMDs) such as MoS$_2$ and WS$_2$. 

These materials are unique in that they are only a few atoms thick, can be readily synthesized on large areas, are highly sensitive to various environmental stimuli (such as strain, light, and temperature), and can be trasnferred cleanly onto many different substrates, creating many different avenues for applications. 

### Radiation Resilience of Nanoscale 2D TMD Transistors

Radiation-hard electronics represent one of the costliest components of spacecraft by mass, and spacecraft manufacturers are evaluating how commercial-off-the-shelf components may reduce cost. Electronic components are often manufactured to radiation-tolerant specifications at chip foundries such as Intel, TSMC, GlobalFoundries, and Micron.

Given that two-dimensional materials are potential candidates for next-generation components, how do 2D-based devices hold up against space radiation?

### Stacked 3D-NAND Transistors for Next-Generation Non-Volatile Memory

Non-volatile memories, such as the memory in standard USB flash drives, have exponentially increased in storage volume due to stacked 3D transistor geometries reducing each cell's areal footprint. 

However, current Charge Trap Flash (CTF) silicon transistor designs limit ultimate scaling by placing a lower bound on gate dielectric and channel material thicknesses, causing higher programming voltages and energy consumption.

We propose that these material limitations can be circumvented by switching the CTF material to an ultrathin ferroelectric HZO, and that the polysilicon channel can be replaced with a 2D semiconductor. 

Can the ferroelectric dielectric-2D semiconductor channel stack surpass the limitations in state-of-the-art CTF-Si technologies? 

### Strain Engineering and Flexible In-Sensor Computing with 2D TMD Transistors

Two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors are atomically thin, allowing them to conform to flexible substrates and experience strain. How does strain impact the electronic and optical properties of 2D semicondctors? Can we engineer their strain properties to improve device performance? 

With the rise of machine learning and generative AI, collecting and processing massive streams of continuous data is crucial for sustaining model fidelity. However, data collection and data processing are traditionally done separately, with passive sensors sending data to a microprocessor, leading to energy inefficiency and data bandwidth limitations. 

Bringing computational power closer to their data sources (e.g. sensors) can facilitate highly optimized, energy-efficient, adaptive sensing systems. Given that 2D semiconductors are strain-sensitive, can we design an energy-efficient integrated sensing-computing system?
